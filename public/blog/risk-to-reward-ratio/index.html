Risk to Reward Ratio in Forex: How to Calculate RR Ratio

By Ali Raza · Axiom Trading Journal · Last updated: October 18, 2026
Quick answer: Risk-to-reward ratio compares how much you risk on a trade (entry to stop-loss) with how much you aim to gain (entry to take-profit). To calculate it, divide the reward distance by the risk distance. A 50-pip stop-loss with a 100-pip target is a 1:2 ratio.

Risk-to-reward ratio (often written as RR or R:R) is one of the first numbers every Forex trader should learn to calculate — and one of the most commonly ignored. It tells you, before you even enter a trade, how much you stand to lose compared to how much you stand to gain. Get this number wrong consistently, and no amount of "good" trade picking will save your account long term.

What Is Risk-to-Reward Ratio in Forex?

Risk-to-reward ratio compares the distance from your entry price to your stop-loss (your risk) against the distance from your entry price to your take-profit (your reward). It is usually written as a ratio like 1:2 or 1:3, meaning you are risking one unit to potentially gain two or three.

Risk-to-Reward Ratio = (Entry Price − Stop-Loss) : (Take-Profit − Entry Price)

How Do You Calculate Risk-to-Reward Ratio? (Example)

Suppose you enter a EUR/USD long position at 1.1000, place your stop-loss at 1.0950, and your take-profit at 1.1100.

Risk = 1.1000 − 1.0950 = 50 pips
Reward = 1.1100 − 1.1000 = 100 pips

Risk-to-Reward Ratio = 50 : 100 = 1:2

In plain terms, you are risking 50 pips to potentially make 100 — for every dollar you could lose on this trade, you stand to gain two if it hits target.

Why Does Risk-to-Reward Matter More Than Win Rate?

New traders often chase a high win rate and ignore risk-to-reward entirely. But the math tells a different story. With a 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio, you can be wrong on more than half your trades and still be profitable overall:

  • Win rate of 40% at 1:2 RR → still net profitable over a large sample of trades
  • Win rate of 60% at 1:0.5 RR (risking more than you aim to gain) → loses money over time, even though you win most trades

This is why professional traders often talk about "risk management" before they talk about "strategy" — the ratio you trade at determines how much room for error your strategy actually has.

What Win Rate Do You Need at Each Ratio?

The break-even win rate (before spreads and commissions) is 1 ÷ (1 + reward multiple). Here is how it changes with your ratio:

Real trading costs (spread, commission, slippage) push the win rate you actually need a little higher than these numbers.

What Is a Good Risk-to-Reward Ratio?

There's no single "correct" risk-to-reward ratio for every trader or every market condition, but a common practical guideline for beginners is to aim for a minimum of 1:1.5 to 1:2 on most setups, and to avoid taking trades where the risk is larger than the potential reward unless the strategy has a proven, well-tested high win rate to compensate.

How Do You Track Risk-to-Reward Consistently?

The hard part isn't calculating risk-to-reward on a single trade — it's tracking it honestly across every trade you take, week after week, so you can see your real average over time. Most traders either skip this entirely or do it manually in a spreadsheet, which tends to fall apart after a few weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate risk-to-reward ratio in forex?

Divide the distance from your entry to your take-profit (reward) by the distance from your entry to your stop-loss (risk). A 50-pip stop and a 100-pip target gives a ratio of 1:2.

What is a good risk-to-reward ratio in forex?

There is no single correct ratio, but many traders aim for at least 1:1.5 to 1:2 on most setups. The right ratio depends on your strategy's win rate.

What win rate do I need at a 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio?

You need to win just over 33% of your trades to break even at 1:2, before spreads and commissions. The break-even win rate is 1 divided by (1 plus the reward multiple).

Is risk-to-reward ratio more important than win rate?

They work together. Win rate and risk-to-reward ratio decide your expectancy, so a lower win rate can still be profitable with a higher ratio, and a high win rate can lose money with a poor one.
